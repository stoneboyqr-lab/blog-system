Why I Stopped Following Tutorials and Started Building Real Systems

L
Livingstone
@lvst_web_dev · Owerri, Nigeria
June 2025 Published
9 min Read Time
0% Read

There's a point every developer hits where tutorials feel hollow. You finish the course, you close the laptop, and you still can't build anything real. I hit that wall hard. I had completed hours of content — and I still couldn't sit in front of a blank editor and build something from scratch without someone holding my hand.

The shift didn't happen overnight. It happened when a pastor walked up to me and said, "Can you build something for our church?" No spec sheet. No Figma file. No YouTube video that covered exactly that use case. Just a real problem that needed a real solution.

"The best thing that ever happened to my growth was someone trusting me before I felt ready."

— Livingstone

The Tutorial Trap

Tutorials are designed to teach you concepts — not to teach you how to think. They give you the path. They never teach you how to find the path yourself. When the tutorial ends, you're left with a clone of someone else's project, zero transferable instincts, and a false sense of completion.

I watched it happen to myself: I'd follow along, everything would work, I'd feel confident — and then the next day I'd try to build something adjacent and freeze. The tutorial had taught me the what without teaching me the why.

The real problem Tutorials optimize for your short-term understanding, not your long-term capability. Every decision is already made for you. In real projects, the decisions are the whole job.

What Building for a Real Client Taught Me

The church management system forced me to make decisions no tutorial prepares you for. What database schema should a sermon collection use? How do you handle an online giving flow where the stakes are real — people's donations? What happens when a user submits a prayer request at midnight and the server goes down?

These aren't tutorial questions. These are engineering questions. And the only way to develop the instinct to answer them is to be in situations where getting them wrong actually matters.

// The Paystack callback that broke everything
// WRONG: async arrow function loses 'this' context
handler.openIframe({
  callback: async (response) => {
    await verifyPayment(response.reference); // ❌ Paystack breaks
  }
});

// RIGHT: regular function works every time
handler.openIframe({
  callback: function(response) {
    verifyPayment(response.reference)
      .then(() => { showSuccess(); })
      .catch((err) => { console.error(err); });
  }
});

That bug cost me two hours. The fix was three characters: removing async. But understanding why — understanding that Paystack's popup library doesn't support async callbacks in its iframe handler — that came from actually being in the trenches, not from a course.

The Stack That Proved Itself

When I started the church project, I didn't know if my backend skills were production-ready. I'd done plenty of practice apps. But this was Node, Express, MongoDB, JWT, Paystack, Multer, and Nodemailer — all wired together, all deployed on Render, all being used by real people sending real money to a real organization.

Every part of that stack taught me something the moment it touched reality:

  • Render's free tier has an ephemeral filesystem — your uploads disappear on every redeploy. You need Cloudinary for persistent file storage.
  • MongoDB Atlas can throw ECONNREFUSED on local networks due to IPv6 DNS resolution. Two lines fix it.
  • Express routes are matched top-to-bottom — your /:slug dynamic route will catch everything above it if placed first.
  • A Mongoose pre-save hook with next() in it will silently double-execute your middleware and corrupt your data.

None of that is in any course I've taken. All of it is seared into my memory because I felt the pain.

What I'd Tell Every Developer At That Wall

Stop waiting until you feel ready. You won't. The feeling of readiness is something that follows shipping, not something that precedes it. Find a real problem — a business, a church, a friend's portfolio, a local shop — and build a real solution for it. Let the gaps show. Fill them in real time. That's the actual curriculum.

Tutorials are the map. Real projects are the territory. At some point you have to put the map down and start walking.
